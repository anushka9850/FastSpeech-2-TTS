FastSpeech 2 Text-to-Speech for English and Telugu

Technical report - implementation, training and variance-adaptor analysis. Datasets: Svarah (English), IndicTTS (Telugu).
Status: results for English, Telugu are not generated yet; sections marked pending fill in automatically when scripts/make_report.py is re-run after preprocessing, training and inference.

1. Implementation approach

The implementation starts from ming024/FastSpeech2 (PyTorch), the reference implementation named in the assignment, and follows the FastSpeech 2 paper (Ren et al., ICLR 2021): a non-autoregressive Transformer that maps phonemes to mel-spectrograms with explicit duration, pitch and energy predictors (the variance adaptor), followed by a pretrained HiFi-GAN vocoder. One shared package, fs2/, holds the model and pipeline; English/ and Telugu/ contain the language-specific preprocess.py, train.py, inference.py and config.yaml, so the two models are trained completely independently.

Figure 1. FastSpeech 2 as implemented. Encoder/decoder FFT blocks, PostNet and HiFi-GAN are taken unchanged from the reference repository.

Components reused from the reference implementation

  • Transformer (fs2/transformer/): FFT blocks (multi-head self-attention + 1-D conv feed-forward, kernel [9, 1]), sinusoidal positions, 4 encoder / 6 decoder layers, hidden 256, 2 heads; the only edit is that vocabulary size comes from the language's symbol table.
  • PostNet: 5 conv layers, 512 channels, residual refinement of the mel.
  • Variance predictors: 2 x (Conv1D-ReLU-LayerNorm-Dropout) + Linear, 256 quantisation bins for pitch and energy embeddings.
  • Loss: L1 on mel before and after PostNet, MSE on log-duration, pitch and energy.
  • Optimiser: Adam (0.9, 0.98, eps 1e-9) with Noam warm-up (4000 steps) and step annealing; gradient clipping 1.0.
  • Vocoder: the reference repo's pretrained HiFi-GAN universal generator (22.05 kHz, 80 mels, fmax 8 kHz); features are computed with the same STFT settings so no vocoder fine-tuning is needed.

2. Datasets, subsampling and preprocessing

English - Svarah (AI4Bharat): 9.6 h of Indian-accented English from 117 speakers across 65 districts, read and extempore speech, released as a single split. No speaker id is published, so a stable pseudo-speaker id is derived from each utterance's speaker metadata (gender, age group, native language, state, district, qualification, job, domain). Telugu - IndicTTS (IIT Madras): studio recordings of one male (4.47 h) and one female (4.27 h) speaker at 48 kHz. Because 6 h cannot come from a single Telugu speaker, both speakers are used with a speaker embedding.

Selection strategy (both languages, fs2/selection.py, seed 1234). (1) keep 1-11 s utterances with a non-empty transcript and no annotation tags; (2) drop the 1% slowest and 1% fastest utterances by characters per second - this removes truncated audio and transcripts that do not match; (3) group utterances with identical normalised text and always place a group in one split, so no sentence occurs in both train and validation (IndicTTS speakers share prompts); (4) give every speaker a share of the 1 h validation and 5 h training budgets proportional to their share of the filtered audio, and fill validation first, then training, visiting groups in seeded random order. Assertions in the code guarantee zero utterance and zero text overlap.

Preprocessing. Resample to 22.05 kHz mono, trim leading/trailing silence (30 dB English, 35 dB Telugu), peak-normalise; text normalisation and G2P (Section 1); 80-bin log-mel (n_fft 1024, hop 256, fmax 8 kHz); F0 with WORLD DIO + StoneMask, unvoiced frames linearly interpolated; frame energy = L2 norm of the STFT magnitude; utterances longer than 1000 frames or with fewer frames than phonemes are discarded; pitch/energy are z-normalised with training-set statistics.

Data. Telugu has two studio speakers reading prepared text; Svarah spreads 6 h over ~117 speakers with varied microphones, accents and partly extempore speech, so each English speaker contributes only a few minutes. Expect the Telugu model to reach a lower mel loss and sound cleaner and more consistent, while the English model averages over speakers (flatter prosody, occasional speaker drift) - a property of the data rather than the architecture.

Front-end. Telugu spelling is close to phonemic, so the rule-based G2P is reliable for native words; its weak points are loan words written in Telugu script (T1: schwa deletion and English vowel qualities are not modelled) and the homograph పట్టు (T2), which no grapheme-level model can resolve. English depends on CMUdict plus a neural G2P: it handles the heteronym pair (E2) through POS tagging, but must guess OOV words (E1 is in CMUdict; 'rhyth', 'andaroo' are not) and is defeated by the malformed token in E3, while the same error in Telugu (T3) is harmless because spaces are not pronounced.

Numbers. Both front-ends expand the date/time sentences (E4, T4) deterministically; Telugu needs inflected forms (వేల vs వేలు, the ordinal పన్నెండవ), which a naive digit-to-word mapping gets wrong.

Limitations. ~5 h per language is about a fifth of LJSpeech, so expect some mumbling on long or rare words, limited prosodic variety and occasional duration errors; the universal HiFi-GAN is not fine-tuned to these voices (slight buzz/metallic artefacts); learned alignments can be imprecise on very short phonemes; global pitch normalisation is shared between male and female voices.

Improvements. (1) fine-tune HiFi-GAN on ground-truth-aligned mels of each corpus; (2) per-speaker pitch normalisation; (3) a Telugu lexicon for English loan words and a context-aware homograph model; (4) input text validation (merge stray fragments, E3); (5) pretrain on a larger multilingual Indic/English set and fine-tune on the 5 h subsets; (6) MFA durations for English to compare with the learned aligner; (7) for Svarah, restrict training to the cleanest read-speech speakers or add a reference encoder for speaker/style.
